\being{abstract}
This report presents a comparative study of pixel-based and region-based loss functions
in semantic segmentation on the navigational dataset for Mars Mission m2020, available in the open
AI4Mars dataset. We implement torch's DeepLabV3+ with a Resnet-50 backbone, using transfer learning
techniques from the default weight, and from a pre-training process on the larger MSL navigational
dataset. By implemented weighted sampling and weighting the loss functions around the underrepresented
big rock class, we are able to achieve accuracy, recall and IoU comparable to other related work.
The final assessment shows that region-based loss functions seem to perform worse than pixel-based
loss function on the m2020 dataset, with the best log cosh dice loss achieving around 60\% mIoU,
and the best cross entropy based model achieving 64\% mIoU.

\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}

Semantic segmentation is a computer vision tool with the task of assigning 
a semantic label to each pixel of an image, or in other terms, 
defining boundaries between different semantic classes \cite{Kale2021}.
Semantic segmentation has been successfully applied in numerous 
different contexts, from medical imaging to self-driving cars \cite{Azad2023, Sultana2020}.

In 2020 NASA has published an open dataset of images with segmentation 
masks from pictures taken by martian rovers as part of the MSL and M2020 missions \cite{Swan2021}.
Numerous groups have evaluated the capacity of different neural networks 
in executing semantic segmentation tasks on the AI4Mars and similar dataset, 
including access time to prediction in the limited hardware of the rovers
\cite{Goutham2022, Atha2022, Luna2025}.

In this work we focus our effort into evaluating the choice of loss 
functions in image segmentation tasks in the m2020 navigational dataset 
from the AI4Mars dataset, containing 2355 images and masks, comparing the effectiveness
of a pixel-based loss function - weighted cross entropy (WCE) - and a region-based
loss function - weighted log cosh dice loss (LCDL) - in segmenting the navigational dataset
from m2020 mars mission.
We also explore the effects of pre-training the model of choice with 
the MSL Navigational dataset, containing 16396 labeled images, a retraining strategy that
provided mixed results in previous works \cite{Swan2021}.

The model chosen was the DeepLav3+ with a ResNet-50 backbone, a model that provides
time to prediction of 25ms and mean accuracy of 87\% when trained and tested in a similar dataset of
martian terrain images by Luna et al \cite{Luna2025}
in an independent dataset of Mars terrain images. We apply this model to the open AI4Mars dataset
to evaluate if the pre training with the MSL and the choice of loss functions can improve the recall
and mean Intersection over Union (mIoU) results in comparison to each other, and related work.

In section 2 we review relevant previous work, both on categorizing different models for
semantic segmentation, as well as previous results of other attempts to do image segmentation on
the AI4Mars or similar independent datasets
In section 3 we present our strategy to process the data and train the model with two different loss
functions - Cross entropy and Log Cosh Dice Loss - and with and without pre training on the MSL dataset.
In section 4, we present the results of our work and discuss them in relation to other relevant works.

\section{Literature Review}

In this section we review two sets of related works - The first review concepts, models and
choice of loss functions in the context of semantic segmentation to motivate our choices in building
our model. In the second subsection we review results of previous related works around their
AI4Mars and similar datasets.

\subsection{Semantic Segmentation}

\subsubsection{Models}

Image segmentation is an image processing technique used to divide an 
image into two or more meaningful regions, or alternatively, the process 
of defining boundaries separating semantic entities in an image. In a 
more technical definition, the task of image segmentation is to assign 
a label to each pixel within an image, with each label connected with 
respect to some property \cite{Ghosh2019}.

Image segmentation can be divided in three categories: Instance 
segmentation, in which different instances of the same semantic label 
are categorized independently; semantic segmentation, which assigns 
different labels for regions with different semantic meaning without 
classifying independent instances independently; and panoptic 
segmentation, which performs both tasks simultaneously \cite{Sultana2020}.

The task of image segmentation has been significantly impacted by the 
development and advancement of deep learning techniques in computer 
vision, in particular after the success of AlexNet in 2012 in using CNN 
for Semantic Segmentation. Since then, numerous Deep Learning models 
for semantic segmentation have been developed with significant 
success \cite{Sultana2020, Kale2021, Minaee2020}.

Some general classes of models for semantic segmentation include 
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), which apply kernel-based 
convolutions to capture the positional relations of features in images. 
By adding pooling and unpooling layers that change the original size, 
it allows CNNs to find meaningful information at multiple scales of 
the feature image by following a ``pyramid-like'' design. CNNs are 
among the most successful approaches to semantic 
segmentation \cite{Minaee2020}.

Another important advancement in machine vision was the development 
of Residual Neural Networks \cite{He2016}, developed as a tool to 
mitigate the degradation that occurred with large neural networks 
by adding the output of earlier layers to the input of deeper layers 
of the network, effectively ``skipping'' some layers. Residual blocks 
allow for deeper networks that do not suffer as much from degradation 
and vanishing coefficients as pure convolutional neural networks.

Another relevant advance in convolutional neural networks is the idea 
of dilated (or ``atrous'') convolutions \cite{Minaee2020, Chen2017}, 
which add an additional parameter to convolutions that increases the 
perceptive field without adding new parameters,according to the dilation rate $r$,
shown in equation \ref{eq:atrous}, where $x$ represents the input and
$w$ represents the kernel. A visual representation of the effects of the
dilution rate in a $3 \times 3$ kernel is shown in figure \ref{fig:atrous}.
In practice, the dilution rate makes the convolution pick samples farther from
the center of the kernel, ``skipping'' neighbors, this increases the field
of view without increasing the Kernel size.


\begin{equation}
  y_i = \sum_{k=1}^K x[i+rk]w[k]
  \label{eq:atrous}
\end{equation}

The basis for the DeepLab family of models are a combination of diluted convolutions,
a pyramid network for enconding the original input, and a CNN for decoding
the final pyramid layer. This combination of  convolution layers and
residual blocks to extract the features of the original
image in multiple scales and stack them together in a multichannel output. In
traditional pyramid networks, this final layer is already close to the output
as shown in figure \ref{fig:pyramid}. The DeepLab model adds complexity by using
the pyramid-like section of it's design to just encode the information of the
original input, the encoded features are then extracted by another CNN structure
from the multi-channel pyramid output, as represented in figur\ref{fig:deeplab}.

\subsubsection{Loss Functions}

Another important choice in implementing a deep learning model is the choice 
of the loss function, a metric that evaluates how far a given output is 
from the expected ground truth. A good choice of loss function can 
mitigate usual challenges of image vision tasks, like noisy datasets 
and unbalanced class representation. 

A number of loss functions are presented and evaluated by the work done 
in \cite{Azad2023}. Here we present the most relevant loss functions for 
our attempt at Semantic Segmentation of the AI4Mars Dataset.

One of the most common loss metrics is the Cross-entropy (CE) loss 
function. It measures the difference between two probability 
distributions given a random variable. In segmentation tasks, this means 
comparing the ground truth with the assigned probability that the pixel
is of that class according to the model (i.e. output of the soft max function).
The formula for the cross entropy is based on Shannon's entropy, and is presented
in a practical formulation in equation \ref{eq:cross_entropy} , $t_n$ is a one-hot
encoded vector that is 1 on the target class and zero on the others, and $\hat{y}$ is the
predicted probability.  In imbalanced datasets, one common approach to minimize the
effects of the imbalance in the training of the model is adding weights that favor the
less represented classes. If $w$ is a vector of the weights of each labeled class,
the weighted cross entropy is presented in equation \ref{eq:weighted_cross_entropy}.


\begin{equation}
  L_{CE} = -\sum_{i=1}^{C} \log(t_i \cdot \hat{y}_i)
  \label{eq:cross_entropy}
\end{equation}


\begin{equation}
  L_{CE} = -\sum_{i=1}^{C} t_i \cdot w \log(t_i \cdot \hat{y}_i)
  \label{eq:weighted_cross_entropy}
\end{equation}

Cross-entropy loss is part of the Pixel level loss functions, this family of loss functions
evaluate each individual pixel as a part of their computation for dissimilarity, and they usually
improve pixel-based assessment. Another approach are region-level loss functions, that focus
on scoring based on the capacity of  a model to adequately separate different semantic regions of
an image. One example of region-level loss function is the Dice Loss function, that scores
similarity based on the intersection between the predicted subset of pixels $Y$ and the ground
truth region $T$, and is written as in equation \ref{eq:dice} for two class classification task, where
$|A|$ represents the size of a set $A$.
Form multi class segmentation tasks, the formula is presented in equation \ref{eq:multiclass_dice},
where $\hat{y}$ represent the prediction probability of each class, and $t$ is the vector that
represents the ground truth. There is a choice in the number of classes considered in the context
of a possible background/negative class, this background class is sometimes excluded from loss computations.

\begin{equation}
  L_{Dice} = 1 - \frac{2 |Y \cap T|}{|Y| + |T|}
  \label{eq:dice}
\end{equation}


\begin{equation}
  L_{Dice} = 1 - \frac{1}{C} \sum_{c=1}^{C} \frac{2 \sum_{i} t_{ic} \hat{y}_{ic}}{\sum_{i} t_{ic} + \sum_{i} \hat{y}_{ic}}
  \label{eq:multiclass_dice}
\end{equation}

A variation of the Dice Loss that offer some advantages in segmentation tasks is the
log cosh Dice Loss, shown in equation \ref{eq:log_dice}, it enhances smoothness and robustness to outliers,
and minimizes the effects of outliers, and it's a better behaved function in terms of derivatives, that helps
the optimization steps. The Dice Loss can also be ``weighted'' in a sense. A generalized version
of the dice loss is defined by the Generalized Wasserstein Dice Loss, the full scope of the
generalization is outside of this work scope, but a less general version of weighted dice loss
was used in one of the training steps, presented in subsection \ref{sec:train}.

\begin{equation}
  L_{log-cosh} = \log(\cosh(L_{Dice})) 
  \label{eq:log_dice}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Martian Terrain Segmentation Related Work}

The task of using computer vision for autonomous driving capability has 
been explored by NASA since 2004 \cite{Swan2021} with the AutoNav project. 
The advancements in computer vision through deep learning models 
presented a great opportunity to improve the autonomous driving 
capabilities of the two active Martian rovers: Curiosity, launched as 
part of the Mars Science Laboratory (MSL) mission, and Perseverance, 
launched as part of the Mars 2020 (m2020) mission. Since advancements
in computer vision fueled by development of deep-learning techniques,
several research groups have attempted to use pictures from martian images,
both from rovers and satellites, as input in segmentation tasks, with goal of
identifying meaningful geological features or terrain for navigation.
\cite{Swan2021, Atha2022, Goutham2022, Luna2025}.
The AI4Mars dataset is just of the datasets explored by this groups, while
others have independent dataset, or have labeled the available images through
different methods, or even implemented some level of semi-supervised learning
algorithms \cite{Goutham2022}.

The AI4Mars navigational dataset divides martian terrain in four classes \cite{Swan2021} :
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Soil}: Good for navigation
\item \textbf{Bedrock}: Ideal for navigation.  
\item \textbf{Sand}: A less favorable but still viable path choice.
\item \textbf{Big Rock}: Must be avoided while choosing a path.
\end{itemize}

The AI4Mars dataset is a publicly available set of images and semantic 
segmentation masks developed through a citizen science initiative by 
NASA. A number of volunteers were trained to create the masks, and 
their work was reviewed by specialist laborers and condensed into the 
final dataset \cite{Swan2021}. One notable challenge in the dataset is that the Big Rock class is 
underrepresented, consisting of less than 2\% of pixels in the m2020 
dataset, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:abd_pie}.

The work by Swan et al \cite{Swan2021} trained a DeepLabV3+ with ResNet-101 on two versions of the
MSL dataset, created by two  different labeling strategies called \textit{random}, that used one possible
mask from multiple suggested by trained labeler, and the \textit{merged} strategy, where the final
masks was a merged version of all suggestion by trained labelers, compiled by an expert on martian
terrain. Their work showed that the model performed significantly better with the \textit{merged}
masks, with the accuracy for big rocks increasing from 82.83\% to 93.24\%. The final confusion

The work by Atha et Al \cite{Atha2022} explores additional machine learning models for
semantic segmentation of martian terrain in the context navigation, exploring how
different models perform both on the MSL and on the M2020 navigational dataset. Their work
identified two main problems with current models that aim to allow for independent path
making for martian rovers. First, models tent to perform worse as the rovers move, and terrain
around them changes naturally, the current models aren't general enough for the martian surface.
Second, the accuracy necessary for a model to perform independently, that is, without a remote human
operator offering input, is significantly higher than the accuracy obtained by models at the time of
that work.

For their model, they trained a ResNet-101 from zero with both MSL and M2020 datasets, they also
trained a ResNet-101 from the weights for ImageNet, and they also tried to transfer train the model
trained on the larger MSL and MER datasets to the M2020 dataset. The transfer learning did improve
performance over soil and rock, but decreased accuracy on labeling bedrock. Their results in
relation to the m2020 Dataset are presented in table \ref{tab:m2020_atha}.

Luna et al \cite{Luna2025} implemented ViBEKO's DeepLabV3+ as part
of their efforts to develop FASTNAV, their tool for independent rover navigation. The dataset used
was independently obtained and labeled by them. Their implementation of DeepLabV3+ with ResNet-50
as a backbone achieved 87\% accuracy and 72\% mIoU with a time to prediction of 25ms, relevant
for practical use in a rover.


\section{Training Method}

\subsection{Dataset and Preprocessing}

The m2020 navigational dataset, consisting of 2355 images with class label masks.
The masks were produced by a crowd science initiative that trained labelers to
identify the four classes: soil, bedrock, sand and big rocks, represented by
pixel color values of 0,1,2,3 respectively. The masks produced by the trained
labelers was reviewed and finalize by a group of expert labelers to produce the final
dataset. The images and masks aren't always the same size, some images
were centercropped before the labels were construct, for this images it's necessary
to center crop the images accordingly when retrieving them from the dataset \cite{Swan2021}.

For training, The dataset is split 
randomly into 90\% for the training set and 10\% for the validation 
set. Both datasets are resized, grayscaled, and converted into 
normalized tensors using the mean and variation suggested by the 
PyTorch implementation of the DeepLabV3+ model \cite{PytorchVision2026}, with
the mean by RGB channel as mean=$[0.485,0.456,0.406]$, with standard deviation of
$[0.229,0.224,0.225]$. 

The training dataset additionally passes through an augmentation loop 
which includes:
\begin{itemize}
\item Horizontal flip (50\% probability): The idea here is that the rover can take
  pictures of the same area viewed from different sides, and the model should
  still be able to perform accordingly.
\item Rotation up to 20° (50\% probability): Some of the rover cameras are free moving
  and may be rotate, so a generalized understanding of the geometry of the classes is important.
\item Random cutouts (up to 8, 50\% probability): Mostly to avoid over training on noise
  due to the limited dataset size
\item Random brightness adjustment (20\% probability): pictures are taken under different
  light conditions in a changing environment like the martian surface.
\end{itemize}

One recurring problem in related works \cite{Atha2022, Goutham2022} was that big rocks
are underrepresented, consisting of only 3.2\% of non-background pixels as shown in
figure \ref{fig:abd_pie}, to compensate a little bit for this during trained, the
data-loader was set with a weighted sampler that favored loading images with big
rock pixels in their mask by a factor of 15.

\subsection{Model & Training: DeepLabV3+ with ResNet-50}
\label{sec:train}

DeepLabV3+ belongs to a family of neural networks that exploits 
dilated (atrous) convolutions to increase the field of view without 
increasing the number of parameters. While Luna et al \cite{Luna2025} was 
successful in implementing DeepLabV3+ on an independent dataset of 
Martian images, this work implements DeepLabV3+ with a ResNet-50 
backbone on the open AI4Mars dataset.

We utilized the PyTorch implementation of DeepLabV3+, which employs 
Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling (ASPP) with an encoder-decoder 
topology \cite{Chen2017}. The training process uses different 
initial learning rates: $0.0005$ for the classifier (ASPP onwards) 
and $0.00001$ for the ResNet-50 backbone. The low learning rates chosen
try to avoid deviating too much from the base model during transfer learning,
since the number of images is relatively low, we are trying to fine tune the default
weights of the model for a new problem, since it's unlikely we can train
a model from zero with so few images.

The model was trained with two possible 
choices of loss function: weighted Cross Entropy Loss, and 
weighted Log-Cosh Dice Loss. The cross entropy loss scores the
model in it's ability to correctly label each pixel, while the Log-Cosh Dice Loss
scores based on the ability to adequately define the correct boundaries of a region. The choice
of the log cosh version of dice loss is due to the big rock class often being present in just
a small section of the image, resulting in fluctuating values for the pure dice loss. The choice
of log-cosh dice loss makes the dice loss a bit more resistant to these variations  \cite{Azad2023}
The weighted cross entropy formula is shown in equation \ref{eq:WCE}, $w_c \in {0.04,0.08,0.15,0.73}$. The log cosh dice loss formula is shown in equation \ref{eq:LCDL} using the same weights. The big rock class was assignment the dominant weight to prevent the gradient from being overwhelmed
by the more abundant classes.

\begin{equation}
  L_{WCE} = - \sum_{c=1}^{C} w_c \cdot y_c \log(\hat{y}_c)
  \label{eq:WCE}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  L_{WLCDL} = log(cosh(1 - \sum_{c=1}^{C} w_c \frac{2 \sum_{i} y_{ic} \hat{y}_{ic}}{\sum_{i} y_{ic} + \sum_{i} \hat{y}_{ic}}))
  \label{eq:LCDL}
\end{equation}

The loss functions was also weighted inversely with the abundance of each class, according
to figure \ref{fig:abd_pie}, so the soil class was weighted as for 4\% of the final loss value,
8\% for bedrock, 15\% for sand and 73\% for big rock. The loss functions also
ignored the background, which in retrospect was a mistake, the final models learned to always guess
a class (usually soil) to minimize their loss, since ``missing'' the background isn't penalized,
as shown in figures \ref{fig:DL_pred} and \ref{fig:CE_pred}.

For each choice of loss function, the model either trained directly from the default weights of the
the torch implementation of DeepLabV3+, or it was initialized from the default weights, then pre trained on the 16396 images of the MSL dataset and then trained and access on the m2020 datasets. This training process
resulted in four different final models with different scores

The optimizer is Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with a decay 
rate of $0.0001$ and momentum of $0.9$. A polynomial scheduler 
is used to dynamically decrease the learning rate with a power of $0.9$.

The model is trained for a total of 150 epochs using a progressive 
resizing strategy:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Epochs 1--50}: $128 \times 128 pixels$ resolution, batch size of 16,
  with two accumulation steps before zeroing the gradient.
\item \textbf{Epochs 51--100}: $256 \times 256 pixels$ resolution, batch size of 8, with 4
  accumulation steps
\item \textbf{Epochs 101--150}: $512 \times 512 pixels$ resolution, batch size of 4 with 4
  accumulation steps.
\end{itemize}
In the last training loop, the model was saved only if the beat the mIoU of the previously saved model,
this early saving was an attempt to minimize over-training, but except for the model trained directly
with cross entropy loss and no pre-training, that performed the best at epoch 34, all other models
were saved around epoch 145-150, suggesting those models could have performed even better given more
training time. The resizing strategy of increasing the image size every 50 epochs
was due to the limited time to train the model, the training time with larger images was
taking a significant while, so that was a compromise to make this project deliverable on time.
It's possible that fully training the model on larger images from that start might provide better
results.


\section{Results}

The evolution of the assessment on accuracy and mean intersection over union (mIoU) of the
four versions of the models with different trained set ups are presented
in figures \ref{fig:mean_acc} and \ref{fig:miou}. The choice of mIoU for model selection
is due to accuracy being a misleading metric over unbalanced classes. A model guessing huge
portions of Big Rocks would get high accuracy on big rocks by chance. The mIoU assessment
metric is better at assessing the models ability to in fact define the boundaries between
the semantic relevant classes.

Regarding the resizing between training epochs, this choice seems to have affected the
Log Likelihood Dice Loss (LCDL) trained models negatively, with the models performing worse
for a significant number of epochs after the resizing, this make sense for a region-based loss
function, that ``cares'' more about the overall structure of the regions. For the cross-entropy
loss trained models, the model seems to quickly adapt to the resized images and improve their
total accuracy and mIoU due to the larger images.

Pre-training on the MSL dataset didn't significantly improve the accuracy and
mIoU of the cross entropy based model, with the model achieving an accuracy of around 80.4\% and
mIoU of 64\% regardless of pretraining, this is consistent with the finding of previous works on
the AI4Mars dataset \cite{Atha2022, Goutham2022}. The findings for the log cosh Dice Loss
trained model is similar in the overall assessment, with maximum accuracy of around 70.7\% and
mIoU of 60\% regardless of pretraining. 

On the ability to classify specific classes, the cross entropy loss trained models seem to
outperform the LCDL  model significantly in the underrepresented big rock class,
achieving around 74\% accuracy and recall on that class, against 57\% on these metrics for
the best performing  dice loss based models.
In the dice loss based models, pretraining HURT the ability of the model in identifying
big rocks, with the accuracy reducing from 57\% to 49\% with pretraining.

It's important to also note a problem in the choice of ignoring the background when computing this losses,
due to this choice, the model learning to ALWAYS guess a class for each pixel, as shown
in figures \ref{fig:DL_pred} and \ref{fig:CE_pred}. The prediction don't contain any background
prediction (colored white on the ground truth), the model was overconfident that anything could
be classified as one terrain, so it even included the sky as a type of soil.

The work by Luna et Al \cite{Luna2025} also trained the DeepLabV3+ with ResNet-50 as a backbone
on an independent but similar dataset to the AI4Mars dataset. Their model achieved 87\%
accuracy and 72\% mIoU. It's not likely we can know where their improvements can from,
if it was due to better choice of loss function and training hyperparameters, or if it was due
to the different dataset.

\section{Conclusion}

In this work we reviewed the literature on Semantic Segmentation tasks applied to martian terrain in the
context of navigational dataset, such as the open AI4Mars Dataset provided by NASA \cite{Swan2021} and
similar datasets, such as the one explored by Luna et al \cite{Luna2025}. A review on models and
loss functions for semantic segmentation \cite{Azad2023, Kale2021, Sultana2020, Minaee2020} motivated
our work in comparing the effect of a choice of pixel-level loss function (Cross Entropy) and a
region-level based loss function (Log Cosh Dice Loss) on segmentation tasks for martian terrain using the
DeepLabV3+ with ResNet-50 backbone.

While this model has been explored in the context of martian terrain segmentation \cite{Luna2025}, it
was not applied to the open AI4Mars dataset. The work by Atha et al \cite{Atha2022} implemented
models with ResNet-101 backbone for AI4Mars segmentation, serving as a good comparison point for
assessment scores. The AI4mars dataset contains two sets of navigational images, from the M2020 mars mission
and from the MSL mission. We trained our model do to image segmentation on the AI4Mars m2020 dataset, based
on masks produced by a crowd science contributions and reviewed by expert labelers \cite{Swan2021}.

The model was trained using a resizing strategy with small batch sizes, but some accumulation steps
for the gradient due to our choice of low learning rates to avoid deviate too much from the default weights
of the model. The work produced four final models, based on the two choices of loss functions: weighted
Cross entropy
or weighted LCDL, both loss functions ignored the background, and the possibility of pre-training the model on the MSL dataset. Our best performing model
overall was the pre-trained cross entropy loss model, achieving a mean accuracy of 80.5\% and
mIoU of 64\%, with a recall on the underrepresented big rock class of 74.2\%.

The final assessment result was lower but consistent with results by Luna et Al \cite{Luna2025}, that achieved
accuracy of 87\% with mIoU of 72\% with their independent navigational dataset. This lower
performance might be due to differences in the dataset, but it also might be to some less than ideal choices.
Further exploration on the exact weights for the weighted loss functions might
have balanced the training better. Also, excluding the background from the loss function proved
to be a mistake, resulting in models that are not capable of identifying when something is not terrain,
as shown in figures \ref{fig:DL_pred} and \ref{fig:CE_pred}.

The assessment results between the pixel-based loss function (Cross entropy) and region-based loss
function (LCDL) suggest that pixel-based loss functions are better suited for segmentation tasks on
navigational datasets of martian terrain, probably due to the fact that big rocks represent small regions,
but are very significant for the segmentation tasks, making region-based loss functions less reliable.
